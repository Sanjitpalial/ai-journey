# Expansion Pack — Semi-Tech & Non-Tech Roles (Communication-Heavy)

Your main roadmap builds the technical trunk. The 4 roles from the last doc (DA/BA/DE/Product Analyst) are technical branches. This doc adds a different kind of branch: roles where **how well you explain something matters as much as whether you can build it.** These are deliberately chosen because they force you to practice communication and presentation — skills that, frankly, every single AI role eventually needs too (you can't get hired or promoted in AI if you can't explain your work to a non-technical stakeholder).

Same logic as before: checkpoint in your main roadmap → small add-on → you're interview-ready.

---

## Group A: Semi-Tech (you still need real technical understanding, but the job is to translate it for someone else)

### 1. Technical / Content Writer (AI & Data niche)
**Ready:** as early as Week 2 — and gets easier with every phase you complete, because you have more to write about.

You write blog posts, documentation, or explainer content about AI/data topics for a company's blog, newsletter, or developer docs. This is a genuinely good side hustle because **you were already going to write notes for yourself to fight forgetting** — just polish those notes into public posts.

**Add-on skills:** clear technical writing (no jargon-soup), structuring an explainer (problem → why it matters → how it works → example), basic SEO awareness if writing for a company blog, knowing how to use AI tools for outlining/editing without losing your own voice (this is now an expected skill for the role, not a shortcut).

**How to practice immediately:** turn every "Sunday Recall Ritual" into a published 400-word post (Medium/Hashnode/LinkedIn). This single habit builds your writing portfolio AND fights forgetting at the same time — it's not extra work, it's the same work published.

**Interview format:** writing sample/test (explain a concept to a beginner in writing), sometimes a live edit task, portfolio review.

---

### 2. Developer Relations (DevRel) / Technical Evangelist
**Ready:** ~Week 10–14 (once you have real ML/DL projects to demo — you need *something* technical to evangelize)

DevRel is "explain technical things to developers, in public, and make them excited." Think conference talks, YouTube tutorials, community Discord/Slack management, sample-app creation.

**Add-on skills:** public speaking (yes, actually speaking, not just writing), building small demo apps that showcase one idea clearly, comfort answering unscripted questions live, community management instincts.

**How to practice immediately:** record yourself explaining one of your projects in 3 minutes, like a conference lightning talk. Watch it back. Painful the first few times — that's the point.

**Resources:** look up any conference talk from a DevRel at a company like LangChain, OpenAI, HuggingFace on YouTube — study their structure, not just their content (notice how they open, how they handle the "wait, why does this matter" question).

**Interview format:** often includes a "give us a 5–10 min talk on a technical topic of your choice" round — this is the whole interview, basically.

---

### 3. Pre-Sales / Solutions Consultant (AI/SaaS products)
**Ready:** ~Week 9–10 (need enough ML/product understanding to speak credibly to a technical buyer)

You sit between sales and engineering — when a company is trying to sell an AI/data product, you're the person who demos it, answers the technical objections, and translates "can it do X" into a real answer.

**Add-on skills:** objection handling, demoing software smoothly without fumbling, translating technical capability into business value ("this isn't just a RAG pipeline, it cuts your support team's research time by half"), basic negotiation/persuasion.

**How to practice immediately:** pick any of your own mini-projects and practice a 5-minute "pitch" of it to a friend or family member who has zero technical background. If they understand why it's useful, you've got it. If their eyes glaze over, redo it.

**Interview format:** mock demo/pitch round, case-style "how would you handle this objection" roleplay, sometimes a take-home where you build a small demo and present it.

---

### 4. QA / Test Analyst (Data & ML systems)
**Ready:** ~Week 5–9 (Python + basic ML understanding is enough — this role tests your attention to detail, not deep ML theory)

You verify that data pipelines or ML model outputs behave correctly — checking edge cases, writing test cases, flagging when a model's predictions look "off." Less glamorous, genuinely good entry point, and teaches you to think like an interviewer would about your own future ML work.

**Add-on skills:** test case design (boundary cases, edge cases), bug-report writing (clear, reproducible, no ambiguity), basic familiarity with how ML model outputs can silently go wrong (data drift, label leakage at a conceptual level).

**Interview format:** "here's a feature/output, find what's wrong with it" practical test, sometimes a written bug-report exercise, behavioral round on attention to detail.

## Group B: Non-Tech (the work is mostly judgment, research, and communication — coding is a bonus, not a requirement)

### 5. Market / Research Analyst
**Ready:** ~Week 5 (Excel + basic stats from Phase 1/2 is already enough)

You research markets, competitors, or trends and turn that into a report or presentation for decision-makers. Almost zero coding; almost all synthesis and communication.

**Add-on skills:** structuring a research report (executive summary first, details after — nobody reads top-to-bottom), basic market-sizing math, building a clean slide deck, citing sources credibly.

**How to practice immediately:** pick any company or product you're curious about and write a 1-page "research brief" on it — market size, competitors, one risk, one opportunity. Do this weekly; it's the entire job, compressed.

**Interview format:** take-home research assignment + presentation of findings, behavioral round.

---

### 6. Associate Project Manager / Project Coordinator
**Ready:** as early as now — this role rewards organization and communication more than any technical skill

You coordinate timelines, track who's doing what, run status updates, unblock people. If you're already tracking your own 5-month roadmap carefully (see the tracker below 👇), you're already practicing the core skill of this job on yourself.

**Add-on skills:** basic Agile/Scrum vocabulary (sprints, standups, backlogs — you don't need certification, just fluency), status-update writing (clear, brief, "here's what's done, what's blocked, what's next"), stakeholder communication.

**Interview format:** behavioral-heavy ("tell me about a time you managed conflicting priorities"), sometimes a prioritization exercise ("here are 6 tasks, you have time for 3, which and why").

---

### 7. Customer Success Manager (SaaS / Data-AI products)
**Ready:** ~Week 5–9 (enough product/data literacy to understand what the customer is actually struggling with)

You help customers actually get value out of a product after they've bought it — onboarding, training, troubleshooting relationship issues, spotting churn risk.

**Add-on skills:** active listening (genuinely the #1 skill here), teaching/onboarding someone patiently, spotting frustration before it becomes a churn risk, writing clear how-to guides.

**Interview format:** roleplay ("here's an unhappy customer, talk me through how you'd handle this call"), behavioral round, sometimes a written communication test (draft an onboarding email).

## The communication/presentation training ground (do this regardless of which role)

These habits build the *underlying* skill that every role above tests in a different costume:

1. **Record yourself weekly** explaining one thing you learned, in under 3 minutes, no notes. Watch it back once. This is uncomfortable and that discomfort is exactly the muscle you're training.
2. **"Storytelling with Data" by Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic** — the book (and her talks on YouTube) is the most practical resource that exists for turning a chart/number into something people actually act on. Directly useful for DA, Product Analyst, Research Analyst, and Pre-Sales alike.
3. **Toastmasters** (free/cheap local chapters almost everywhere, including most Indian cities) — genuinely the most structured way to get repeated public-speaking practice with feedback, if you want a real accountability structure for this specific skill.
4. **Publish weekly**, even if almost nobody reads it (LinkedIn post or a short blog). Writing publicly forces the kind of clarity that writing privately never does — you'll over-explain in your diary and under-explain in public, and that gap is exactly what you need to close.
5. **The Pyramid Principle** (Barbara Minto) — old consulting framework, but it's the single best mental model for "say the conclusion first, then the supporting reasons" — directly transferable to every interview, presentation, and status update you'll ever give.

## Order-of-attack suggestion across everything (both docs combined)

If you want one sequence to actually follow instead of juggling 11 options:

**Week 5:** Data Analyst + Business Analyst + Research Analyst + Project Coordinator (all reachable now, pick 1–2, not all 4)
**Week 9–10:** Product Analyst + QA/Test Analyst + Customer Success Manager
**Week 10–14:** DevRel + Pre-Sales/Solutions Consultant (once you have real projects to demo)
**Ongoing from Week 2:** Technical Writer (this one never "ends," just keep publishing)
**Week 10–14 (parallel, biggest lift):** Data Engineer

Don't chase all 11. Pick 2–3 that genuinely interest you, get good at presenting yourself for those, and let the rest stay optional background knowledge.
